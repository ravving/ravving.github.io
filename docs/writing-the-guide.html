    Writing the guide

    How to write the pages people read: voice, organisation, paragraphs, lists and steps, with the research behind it.

    How to write the pages people read: the manual, the webpage's FEATURES and SHORTCUTS, and the README. The rules come from plain-language and documentation research (sources at the end); tools/check-guide-style.py checks the ones a script can measure.

    Voice

    • Talk to the reader. Write "you", in the active voice: "Hold the step key for half a second and it latches." Not "a step key held past half a second latches its pad".
    • Easygoing, not vague. Friendly and relaxed, with the right musical terms as musicians say them: bar, beat, swing, quantise, root, scale, velocity, accent, cutoff, envelope, send.
    • Only what the app does. Check every term and claim against the code or the API reference before writing it. No guessing, no comparisons from the requests that created a feature, no design references, no history, no internal names (classes, files, PipeWire, JACK).

    Words

    • Concrete, concise, familiar words. "Hold the key for half a second", not "sustain the input briefly". Plain everyday words over jargon; a musical term only where it is the right one. No filler ("simply", "just", "very", "in order to").
    • Inclusive and constructive. Gender-neutral (you, they), and phrased to help: say what to do ("Tap LOCK to edit while it plays") rather than what went wrong.
    • Casual on purpose. Writing courses prefer formal language for academic texts; these pages are not academic, so they stay relaxed and friendly, never sloppy.
    • On-screen names as the screen shows them, in capitals (SELECT, LOCK, SOUND EFFECTS), and the device's keys as printed (MENU, ALT, MODE, BACK, PLAY, STOP).

    Organisation

    • Focus and unity. Each section and each paragraph is about one thing, and every sentence in it serves that thing. A sentence that belongs elsewhere moves there.
    • Pick the order that fits the content. Steps and anything that happens over time go in time order (first, then, finally). Features and options go in order of importance: what most people need first, special cases last. The screen or the device's front panel is described in space order (top to bottom, left to right).
    • One page per job. Keep the four kinds apart: a first walk-through (learning), how to do one thing (a task), what every key or knob does (reference), and why it works this way (explanation). Link between them instead of mixing them in one section.

    Headings and order

    • Start from what the reader wants to do, not from the screen's layout: "Move notes after you've written them", not "The SELECT button".
    • Most important first. The first sentence of a section says what it is for; details and exceptions come after.
    • A heading every 100–200 words. Readers scan the headings and the left edge of the text. So make each heading say what the section does ("Save your project"). Start paragraphs and list items with the words that carry the meaning.

    Paragraphs and sentences

    • One idea per paragraph, one to three sentences. A paragraph over about 60 words wants a list, a table or a split.
    • One idea per sentence, about 20 words, never over 35. A semicolon chain or a third parenthesis is a sign to split.
    • Link the sentences. Each one follows from the last: repeat the key word, or use a signal word that says how it connects. Time: "first", "then", "finally". Adding: "also". An example: "for example". A contrast: "but", "instead". Place on the screen: "above", "below", "at the right". A list keeps its items the same shape.
    • Short paragraphs on purpose. Writing courses ask four sentences or more of an essay paragraph. A manual on a screen is scanned instead, so its paragraphs stay at one to three sentences, and lists, tables and the next heading carry the rest.

    Lists, steps and tables

    • Steps are numbered, one action each, starting with a verb, and say what happens: "1. Tap LOCK. The grids accept edits again."
    • Parallel facts are a bulleted list, each item bold on its first words when the list is long.
    • Keys, knobs and settings are a table: what you press, what it does, its range.
    • No more than one level of nesting.

    Look

    • Lines of 50–75 characters. The docs column is capped at 70ch.
    • White space between blocks. Short paragraphs, a blank line before and after lists and tables.
    • Screenshots only where they help a step, and retaken when the screen changes.

    Mechanics

    Grammar, punctuation and spelling are part of being trusted. Before committing a page:

    • Proofread it once for meaning and once for typos.
    • Read it aloud: a sentence you run out of breath on is too long.
    • Run the spelling pass (below) when a British English dictionary is installed: ravving's pages spell British (colour, quantise, organise).

    Checked by a script

    python3 tools/check-guide-style.py reports, for the user pages: sentences over 35 words, paragraphs over 60 words, sections over 200 words without a heading, and lines that chain more than two semicolons. Run it before committing a guide page. --spell adds a spelling pass with hunspell's en_GB dictionary (install hunspell-en_gb), skipping code, key names and words in capitals.

    Sources

    • Federal Plain Language Guidelines and the archived plainlanguage.gov quick tips: "you", active voice, sentences around 20 words, short sections, many useful headings.
    • Australian Government Style Manual: plain language.
    • Nielsen Norman Group's eye-tracking work on scanning, as summarised by Newcastle University's readability guidance and UXPin: most readers scan; headings and the left edge carry the page.
    • Diátaxis, as adopted by Canonical: tutorials, how-to guides, reference and explanation kept apart.
    • Google developer documentation style guide: procedures: numbered steps, an imperative introduction, shallow nesting.
    • How to format a blog post for readability and I'd Rather Be Writing: lines of 50–75 characters, white space, paragraphs of one to three sentences.
    • The properties of a well-written text, as taught in writing courses: the Quizlet study guide "Key Properties of a Well-Written Text" and an overview.
    • Organisation: focus, unity, development and patterns.
    • Coherence and cohesion: signal words of time, sequence, space, illustration and contrast.
    • Language use: concrete, concise, familiar and inclusive words.
    • Mechanics: proofreading, reading aloud, grammar and spell checkers.
